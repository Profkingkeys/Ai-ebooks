# 23 · Building Wealth with AI: Start with a Customer, Not a Millionaire Promise

**The first useful question is not “How do I become a millionaire?” It is “Whose expensive problem can I solve well enough that they will pay?”**

AI can reduce the effort needed to prototype, analyze and deliver certain services. It does not guarantee demand, profit or wealth. This guide turns an ambitious income goal into a business experiment. It is not financial advice, and the numerical examples are invented planning exercises rather than earnings forecasts.

## Setup and model choice

List three skills you can demonstrate and three groups whose work you understand. Look for a repeated, costly task: reconciling inventory, preparing accessible learning materials, organizing authorized business records or maintaining a small company's website. Interview potential users about what they do now, what fails and who controls the budget. Do not begin by asking whether they like your AI idea.

Use a text model to organize interview notes and identify unanswered questions, a coding model to build a narrow prototype, and deterministic calculations for unit economics. Remove personal or confidential information before sharing notes. The [model guide](31-models-and-workspace.md) explains how to compare capabilities without buying every available subscription.

## Test a specific offer

Write an offer with a deliverable, deadline, scope, acceptance criteria and support boundary. For example: “I will turn your authorized inventory exports into a reproducible monthly quality report, with validation rules and a documented handover.” Avoid claiming savings you have not measured. A paid pilot can test willingness to pay more meaningfully than compliments on a demo.

Suppose a hypothetical service charges 100 units of currency per project. Delivery labor costs 45, tools cost 10 and payment/other variable costs total 5. The contribution before fixed overhead is 40, not 100. If support doubles your labor, the economics change. Include your own time even when you are the founder; ignoring it can disguise an unsustainable service.

| Question | Evidence | Decision |
|---|---|---|
| Is the problem costly? | Actual workflow and failure examples | Choose a narrow pain point |
| Will someone pay? | A legitimate paid pilot | Validate the offer |
| Can you deliver reliably? | Acceptance checks and support log | Improve or reduce scope |
| Does it make money? | Revenue minus all relevant costs | Reprice or redesign |
| Can it repeat? | Similar needs across customers | Standardize carefully |

## Build a business asset

Document the steps you repeat, then automate the stable parts. Keep human review where mistakes are expensive. A template, validated data pipeline or well-supported product can become more reusable than one-off prompting. Avoid copying client confidential material into a public portfolio; use permissioned case studies or synthetic examples.

Ask AI: “Critique this offer from the buyer's perspective. Identify unclear scope, hidden support costs, weak evidence and reasons the buyer may prefer their current process. Do not invent market-size figures, testimonials or customer traction.” Treat the critique as hypotheses to test with real conversations.

## Practical project and challenges

Run a thirty-day experiment: choose a user group, conduct a small set of genuine interviews, build one demonstrable solution, seek a properly scoped pilot and document the outcome. Track responses, conversion, delivery time, rework and customer satisfaction. Follow applicable registration, tax, consumer and data-protection requirements in your jurisdiction with qualified advice where needed.

If nobody buys, investigate the problem, buyer, timing and offer before adding features. If every client needs a different system, a service business may fit better than software subscriptions. If tool costs erase the margin, simplify the workflow and compare model options with the same evaluation set.

The long-term path to wealth involves useful work, reliable delivery, ownership, financial discipline and uncertainty. A credible portfolio shows real outcomes and honest lessons. Let the ambition motivate consistent improvement; do not let it push you into fake claims, speculative debt or promises to customers that the product cannot keep.
